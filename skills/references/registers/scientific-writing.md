# Scientific and academic writing register

Use this reference when editing manuscripts, abstracts, methods, results,
discussions, grant narratives, literature reviews, peer-review responses, or other
research prose. The calling skill determines editorial authority:

- Faithful Humanizer treats this file as preservation constraints.
- Editorial Humanizer uses it as genre-specific guidance within its factual and
  epistemic boundaries.

This reference does not authorize fact checking, source invention, stronger claims,
or a more conversational voice.

## Preserve scientific meaning

Protect all of the following unless the user explicitly authorizes a substantive
change:

- technical and disciplinary terms, symbols, abbreviations, definitions, units,
  variable names, and outcome names;
- repeated exact terminology when synonym variation would reduce precision;
- modality, qualification, uncertainty, limitations, and evidence boundaries;
- negation, scope, comparison groups, conditions, exclusions, and time horizons;
- correlation, association, prediction, explanation, and causation as distinct
  relations;
- statistical direction, magnitude, uncertainty, significance language, and
  reported thresholds;
- citations, citation placement, attribution, quotations, and the boundary between
  an author's interpretation and a cited source's claim;
- methods order, procedural sequence, agent visibility, and reproducibility details;
  and
- established genre structure when order carries scientific meaning.

Do not strengthen `may`, `might`, `suggests`, `appears`, `is consistent with`, or
`is associated with`. Do not turn an observed association into a cause. Do not
remove a limitation merely because it weakens the conclusion.

## Passive voice and agency

Passive voice is conventional and useful when the procedure, sample, or result is
more important than the actor, especially in methods and results sections. Preserve
it when the actor is unknown, intentionally backgrounded, conventional, or already
clear.

Change passive voice only when the revision improves clarity without inventing or
changing agency. Never change `samples were measured` to `we measured the samples`
unless the source establishes that `we` performed the measurement.

## Terminology and repetition

Precision outranks lexical variety. Repeat the same technical term when it denotes
the same construct. Do not cycle through near-synonyms for rhythm if they could imply
different variables, populations, mechanisms, or levels of analysis.

Plain-language substitutions are appropriate only when they remain exact for the
audience and task. Preserve a field's accepted term when a simpler word would be
broader, narrower, or less reproducible.

## Hedging and qualification

Distinguish redundant hedge stacking from legitimate epistemic caution. A phrase
such as `could potentially possibly` may contain removable duplication. A single
`may`, `suggests`, confidence interval, limitation, or scope qualifier may be
scientifically load-bearing.

Do not delete a hedge solely to make prose more direct. Do not add a hedge that
changes an author's stated confidence. Preserve the owner and target of every
qualification.

## Citations and attribution

Never invent a citation, author, study, dataset, journal, or result. Keep citation
markers and their associated claims together. Do not convert `Smith et al. reported`
into an unattributed fact.

In Editorial mode, vague attribution may be removed, generalized, or flagged with a
request for a source when the task permits. In Faithful mode, it remains vague and
attributed because evaluating its support would change the supplied content.

## Contextual editorial signals

The following may merit editing, but none is an automatic error:

- empty significance announcements that do not state the scientific implication;
- redundant discussion paragraphs that restate the same result;
- formulaic limitation pivots or generic positive conclusions;
- superficial participial phrases such as an unsupported `highlighting the
  importance of` clause;
- vague attribution that needs a citation;
- metadiscourse that announces rather than advances the argument; and
- repeated rhetorical templates that obscure the evidence chain.

Judge these signals by density, genre, intention, and meaning. Preserve conventional
language when it is doing real scientific work.

## Register and tone

Maintain the formality expected by the document and field. Do not make an abstract,
methods section, manuscript, grant, or peer-review response conversational merely
to make it sound human. Natural scientific prose can remain formal, technical,
cautious, repetitive, and passive where those choices improve precision.

Punctuation, heading capitalization, lists, and citation style should follow the
author, venue, or supplied style guide. An em dash, semicolon, three-item list,
Oxford comma, or title-case heading is not an AI signal by itself.

## Final register check

Before returning an edit, verify that:

- every scientific claim keeps its original force and evidence boundary;
- no actor, mechanism, result, citation, or implication was invented;
- technical terms still denote the same constructs;
- statistical and causal meanings are unchanged unless the user explicitly
  authorized substantive correction; and
- the result reads naturally for its scientific genre rather than being pushed
  toward generic conversational prose.
